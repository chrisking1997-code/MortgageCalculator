When interest rates move, mortgage decisions need even more care. Whether you are in Taiwan, the US or the UK, the core question is the same: **finding the balance between cash-flow flexibility and total interest cost.**

Many people judge a mortgage only by "Can I afford the monthly payment?", and that is exactly the most common trap. This article uses real numbers to walk through the key choices.

## 1. Don't be fooled by the monthly payment: term and total interest

A longer term lowers the monthly payment, but total interest rises sharply. For a **NT$10 million loan at 2.2%**, repaid in equal monthly instalments:

| Term | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 20 years | about NT$51,541 | about NT$2.37 million |
| 30 years | about NT$37,970 | about NT$3.67 million |
| 40 years | about NT$31,345 | about NT$5.05 million |

Going from 30 to 40 years cuts the payment by only about NT$6,600 a month but adds about **NT$1.38 million** in interest.

That doesn't make long terms bad. A longer term gives breathing room each month, and if you save or plan the difference you keep flexibility and can prepay later. The point is to **know exactly what that flexibility costs.**

## 2. What share of income is safe?

A common rule of thumb is that **the mortgage payment should not exceed one third of household monthly income**. A payment of NT$38,000, for example, suggests a household income of at least NT$114,000.

That is just a starting point. Also consider:

- Other debts such as car loans or personal loans
- Household size and children's education costs
- Income stability (sales and self-employed incomes vary more)
- Whether you have an emergency fund of at least six months

## 3. Taiwan's grace period: tempting, but not free

Taiwanese banks often offer a grace period during which you pay interest only. For buyers who need cash for renovations, or whose finances are tight in the short term, it provides breathing space.

But when the grace period ends, the principal must be repaid over fewer years and the payment jumps. Using the NT$10 million, 2.2%, 30-year example with a three-year grace period:

- First three years: interest only, about **NT$18,333** a month
- From year four: the principal must be cleared in 27 years, so the payment rises to about **NT$41,000**, higher than the NT$37,970 without a grace period

Unless you have a clear use for the money and are sure you can afford the later payment, don't take a grace period just because the first few years look easier. For a fuller model see [Grace periods: poison or cure?](/blog/en-grace-period-arbitrage/).

## 4. Stress-test for rising rates

Most Taiwanese mortgages are **floating-rate** and move with central bank policy. Before buying, run a stress test: could you still pay if rates rose?

For the same NT$10 million over 30 years:

- At 2.2%: about **NT$37,970** a month
- At 3.2%: about **NT$43,247** a month

A 1 percentage point rise adds about NT$5,300 a month, more than NT$60,000 a year. In our [mortgage calculator](/mortgage), add 1 to 2 points to the rate and check you still have room in the worst case.

## 5. The US: fixed rates and extra payments

Most US mortgages are 15- or 30-year fixed-rate loans, so the rate never changes. In that system, **extra payments** are the most direct way to cut total interest: every extra dollar of principal reduces the interest on every later payment.

On a $400,000, 6.5%, 30-year loan, paying an extra $300 a month clears it about seven and a half years early and saves around $150,000 in interest.

Before prepaying, consider:

- **Opportunity cost:** if your mortgage rate is low, investing spare money may earn more over time; but investing is risky, while the "return" on prepaying is certain.
- **Liquidity:** once you repay the bank, getting the money back is hard, so make sure your emergency fund is adequate.
- **Penalties:** some loans charge for early repayment in the first few years, so check your contract.

## 6. The UK: remortgage before the fixed deal ends

UK mortgages commonly come with two- or five-year fixed introductory rates. When the deal ends, borrowers who don't remortgage move onto the lender's standard variable rate (SVR), which is usually much higher. Most homeowners start comparing deals three to six months before the end date. See [Bank of England raises rates to 4%](/blog/en-uk-base-rate-remortgage/).

## 7. A checklist before you apply

1. Calculate the monthly payment and total interest with our [mortgage calculator](/mortgage), and stress-test a rate rise.
2. Check the payment as a share of household income, and that your emergency fund is sufficient.
3. Compare at least three lenders on rate, fees, tie-in period and early repayment terms.
4. Be clear about what a grace period is for, and what the payment will be afterwards.
5. If you are still deciding between buying and renting, compare long-term net worth with our [rent vs buy calculator](/rent-vs-buy).

A mortgage is the largest debt most people ever take on. An afternoon spent running the numbers can save tens or even hundreds of thousands in interest. Compare several lenders before applying, and for personal investment or tax decisions, consult a licensed professional.
