> "When will my money actually double?" is one of the most common questions new investors ask. Rather than relying on gut feeling or hot tips, learn the finance world's classic shortcut: the Rule of 72. Once you understand it, you can see the effect of inflation and the power of compounding in seconds.

## 1. What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate how long it takes for money to double:

**72 ÷ annual return (%) ≈ years needed to double**

For example, at 6% a year, 72 ÷ 6 = 12, so roughly 12 years. No calculator needed.

It works because of compounding: each year's return is added to the principal and earns returns the following year. The exact doubling time needs logarithms, but 72 gives a very small error over common return ranges and divides neatly by many numbers, which is why it is so widely used.

## 2. How accurate is it?

| Annual return | Rule of 72 estimate | Exact doubling time |
|---|---|---|
| 1% | 72 years | 69.7 years |
| 2% | 36 years | 35.0 years |
| 4% | 18 years | 17.7 years |
| 6% | 12 years | 11.9 years |
| 7% | 10.3 years | 10.2 years |
| 8% | 9 years | 9.0 years |
| 10% | 7.2 years | 7.3 years |
| 12% | 6 years | 6.1 years |

Between 4% and 12% the rule is almost exact. At very low returns (such as 1%) it slightly overstates the time, but it is still good enough for a quick judgement.

## 3. A stark comparison: time deposits vs. a market ETF

- **Bank time deposit (assume 2% a year):** 72 ÷ 2 = 36 years. NT$1 million takes about 36 years to become NT$2 million, and by then inflation will have eaten a large share of that NT$2 million's purchasing power.
- **Broad market ETF (assume 7% a year over the long run):** 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3 years. The same NT$1 million could double in about 10 years. Over 30 years it might double nearly three times (1 → 2 → 4 → 8 million).

Note that **7% is a long-run average assumption, not a guarantee.** Stock returns vary widely from year to year and can fall for several years in a row, so doubling may take longer than the estimate.

## 4. The rule also tells you when inflation halves your money

Use it in reverse to estimate how long it takes inflation to halve purchasing power:

**72 ÷ inflation rate (%) ≈ years until purchasing power halves**

- 2% inflation: about 36 years
- 3% inflation: about 24 years

If you are 35 today and inflation runs at 3%, by 59 your NT$1 million will buy only about half as much. See year-by-year figures in our [inflation calculator](/inflation).

## 5. Real return: the speed at which you actually get richer

Subtract inflation from your return to get the real return, then apply the rule to see how fast your *purchasing power* doubles:

- 2% deposit − 2% inflation = 0% real return: purchasing power **never doubles**.
- 7% ETF − 2% inflation ≈ 5% real return: purchasing power doubles in about **14 to 15 years**.

That is why long-term money in time deposits often just stands still rather than building wealth.

## 6. Limits of the Rule of 72

1. **It only applies to a single lump sum.** Most people invest monthly, where the rule does not apply directly.
2. **It assumes a fixed return.** Real markets fluctuate, and the order of returns also matters.
3. **It ignores fees and taxes.** Management fees, trading costs and dividend tax all lengthen the real doubling time.

## 7. What about monthly investing?

To find out how long a lump sum plus monthly contributions takes to reach a target, you need a month-by-month calculation. For example:

- Starting amount: NT$1 million
- Monthly contribution: NT$10,000
- Assumed annual return: 7%

It takes about **21 years** to reach NT$10 million. Enter your own figures in our [compound interest calculator](/compound) for a year-by-year breakdown, or use the [DCA calculator](/dca-calculator) to see how much comes from contributions versus growth.

## Conclusion

The real value of the Rule of 72 is not precision, but showing you **how large the gap between returns really is**: 2% and 7% sound only five points apart, yet the doubling times differ by more than three times.
