People often share their FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) targets with me. A typical case: current spending of about NT$80,000 a month, and by the 4% rule, NT$24 million seems enough to retire safely.

I usually ask just one question: **"How many years from now do you plan to retire? Ten? Fifteen? Are you sure NT$80,000 will still buy the same lifestyle then?"**

Most people pause. This is the most common blind spot in FIRE planning: **we are used to defining future freedom in terms of today's purchasing power.**

## A refresher: how the 4% rule works

The 4% rule comes from US research on retirement withdrawals: withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust the amount for inflation each year. Over most historical 30-year periods, the money did not run out.

As a target, that means:

> Financial independence target = annual spending × 25

At NT$80,000 a month, that is NT$960,000 a year, times 25 = **NT$24 million**.

The catch is that "annual spending" should be your spending **in the year you retire**, not today's.

## The underestimated "negative compounding": inflation before you retire

Everyone likes calculating how much a 7% ETF return can grow into, but often forgets that inflation compounds too, only in the opposite direction.

Suppose you are 35 and plan to retire at 50 (in 15 years), and you currently need NT$80,000 a month. If inflation runs at 2.5% a year (given recent rises in food and rent, this is not an extreme assumption):

- In 15 years you would need about **NT$116,000** a month for the same lifestyle.
- Plugging that into the 4% rule gives a target of about **NT$34.76 million**, not NT$24 million.

That gap of more than NT$10 million is one reason some early retirees find their money running short and return to work.

Targets under different inflation assumptions:

| Annual inflation | Monthly spending in 15 years | Retirement target (× 25) |
|---|---|---|
| 0% (inflation ignored) | NT$80,000 | NT$24.0 million |
| 2% | about NT$108,000 | about NT$32.3 million |
| 2.5% | about NT$116,000 | about NT$34.76 million |
| 3% | about NT$125,000 | about NT$37.39 million |

## What this does to your plan

Suppose you already have NT$5 million invested, can add NT$60,000 a month, and assume a 7% annual return:

- Aiming for NT$24 million: about **11.6 years**.
- Aiming for about NT$34.76 million: about **15.5 years**.

Same person, same effort, and nearly four years' difference in retirement date, purely from whether the target accounts for inflation.

## Two ways to build inflation into the plan

**Method 1: convert the target into future money.** As in the table, estimate your spending in the year you retire and multiply by 25. Our [inflation calculator](/inflation) shows how today's amount changes over 15 years.

**Method 2: use a real return.** Keep the target in today's money (NT$24 million), but subtract inflation from your investment return. A 7% nominal return with 2.5% inflation gives a real return of about 4.4%. Projections made this way are expressed in today's purchasing power.

The two methods give very similar answers. What matters is **never combining a nominal return with today's spending as the target**, which badly underestimates what you need.

## Extra things early retirees should consider

1. **A longer retirement.** The 4% rule was studied over 30 years. If you retire at 50 and need the money to last past 90, many people use a 3% to 3.5% withdrawal rate instead, meaning a target of 28 to 33 times annual spending.
2. **Market risk early in retirement.** A crash just after you retire does disproportionate damage (sequence risk). Keeping one or two years of spending in low-risk assets is a common buffer.
3. **Healthcare and insurance costs**, which usually rise with age.
4. **The gap before pensions start.** Early retirees must wait until the statutory age to claim Taiwan's labour insurance and labour pension, so that gap needs separate funding. See our Chinese article on [voluntary labour pension contributions](/blog/labor-pension-voluntary-contribution/).

## Stop guessing and let the numbers speak

Before starting any retirement plan:

1. Use the [inflation calculator](/inflation) to see what your current spending becomes in the year you retire.
2. Use the [FIRE calculator](/fire) with your income, spending and current assets, and set the scenarios with a real return to see how many years you really need.
3. Use the [compound interest calculator](/compound) to check whether your monthly contributions are enough.

Over the long run your assets need to grow faster than inflation, or financial freedom stays a number on paper. The numbers can be harsh, but only by facing them can you make a plan that truly fits.
