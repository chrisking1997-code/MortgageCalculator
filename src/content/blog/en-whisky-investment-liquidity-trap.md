People often ask me: "K, the stock market is at a high and inflation is eating my cash. Should I buy a few casks of Scotch, or collect some Karuizawa and Macallan bottles to preserve value?"

Dealers and auction houses love to call whisky "liquid gold" and show charts of a bottle that multiplied in value over ten years.

I have researched and allocated into whisky casks myself (the process is in [I bought a cask of whisky](/blog/whisky-cask-investment/), in Chinese), so I'll be candid: **whisky can be a small part of an allocation, but if you buy it with a stock-trading mindset, liquidity and transaction costs can eat most of your gains.**

## The hidden costs you didn't count

With shares, you open an app and sell at market price in seconds, usually for a fraction of a percent. Physical assets play by completely different rules.

Many people see a bottle fetch NT$100,000 at auction and assume the same bottle in their cabinet can be turned into NT$100,000 at any time. That is the liquidity illusion.

### 1. High transaction costs
Selling through an auction house usually means a seller's commission plus listing, insurance and shipping fees. These vary widely by platform, but a combined 10% to 15% is common, and the buyer pays a separate buyer's premium.

What does that mean? If 15% is deducted when you sell, your bottle must rise about **17.6%** on paper just to break even:

> Rise needed to break even = 1 ÷ (1 − fees) − 1
> 1 ÷ (1 − 15%) − 1 ≈ 17.6%

If your purchase price already included a dealer's markup, the hurdle is even higher.

### 2. A long time to sell
From deciding to sell, contacting an auction house, authentication, shipping and listing to the hammer falling and the money arriving often takes several months. When you need cash urgently, this "liquid gold" can't help; selling privately in a hurry usually means accepting a much lower price. That is the **liquidity discount**.

### 3. Holding costs
- **Bottles:** must be kept upright, out of light and at a stable temperature. Condition (fill level, label, box) directly affects price.
- **Casks:** annual storage and insurance fees, plus the natural evaporation known as the "Angel's Share".

All of these come straight off your net value every year.

### 4. Opaque pricing
Shares have real-time public prices. Whisky "prices" mostly come from sporadic auction results, and the same bottle can sell for very different amounts at different times and on different platforms. You often don't know what yours is worth until you actually sell.

## Paper gains vs real gains: a simple example

Suppose you buy a bottle for NT$50,000 and five years later its "market price" is NT$70,000: a 40% paper gain that looks great.

But if 15% in fees is deducted when you sell, you actually receive about **NT$59,500**: a 19% gain over five years, or about 3.5% a year. After five years of storage costs, that may be similar to a deposit or a bond, with far more risk.

The same NT$50,000 invested at 7% a year would be about NT$70,000 after five years. Model your own scenarios with our [compound interest calculator](/compound).

## Where whisky reasonably fits in a portfolio

So should you never touch whisky? Not necessarily. The key is **getting its role right**.

The main feature of physical alternative assets like whisky is their **relatively low correlation with stocks and bonds**, which can add some diversification. But low correlation is not the same as "can't fall": alternative asset markets have their own cycles, and when they cool, prices can stagnate for years and buyers can be hard to find.

**My own rules for alternative assets:**

- **Allocation:** alternative assets in total no more than 5% to 10% of total assets.
- **Type of money:** strictly spare money, meaning money that, even if it couldn't be sold at all in the next 5 to 10 years, would not affect your life or cash flow.
- **Core first, satellites second:** only consider alternatives once your emergency fund and core investments are in place.

## Five questions before you buy

1. Will I need this money in the next 5 to 10 years?
2. Do I know how much dealer or middleman markup is in the price?
3. How will I sell it, what will it cost, and how long will it take?
4. Can I accept opaque pricing, and possibly not being able to sell for years?
5. Without any whisky, is my current allocation already enough to cope with inflation?

Ignore get-rich stories and go back to the numbers. Use our [inflation calculator](/inflation) to see how much your cash will shrink, then check your allocation with the [portfolio tool](/portfolio-roi). If your existing stocks and bonds already cover inflation, there is no need to buy whisky just because others are.

**A final warning:** many whisky and cask schemes are marketed with "guaranteed returns" or "can't lose" promises. Treat any physical-asset investment that guarantees high returns as a red flag, and verify ownership documents and storage location carefully before deciding.
