Is the mortgage grace period a tool for speculators or a lifeline for first-time buyers? From a financial-engineering point of view it is a double-edged sword: the outcome depends almost entirely on what you do with the cash it frees up.

This article skips the opinions and runs the numbers: **when does investing the money saved during a grace period pay off, and when does it backfire?**

> This is a mathematical illustration, not investment advice. All returns are assumptions, and real markets go up and down.

## What a grace period really does: trades time for cash flow

During a grace period you **pay interest only, not principal**. In Taiwan, ordinary mortgages usually offer one to three years; some government-backed loans (such as the "New Youth Housing Loan") allow up to five.

Take a **NT$15 million mortgage at 2.1% over 30 years**:

| | No grace period | Five-year grace period |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment, years 1–5 | about NT$56,196 | about NT$26,250 (interest only) |
| Monthly payment from year 6 | about NT$56,196 | about NT$64,311 |
| Total interest over 30 years | about NT$5.23 million | about NT$5.87 million |

The grace period frees up about **NT$30,000 a month** for the first five years. It comes at two costs:

1. **From year six, the payment is about NT$8,100 higher than without a grace period**, because none of the NT$15 million principal has been repaid and it must now be cleared in 25 years.
2. **Total interest rises by about NT$640,000**, because the principal is repaid later.

## The spread trade: what if you invest the savings?

The idea behind the "spread trade" is that if you consistently invest the roughly NT$30,000 saved each month in assets that return **more than your mortgage rate** (such as a broad market ETF), the investment gains may exceed the extra interest.

The fair comparison is **net worth at the end of year five**:

- **A (no grace period):** pays the extra NT$30,000 a month into principal; after five years the loan balance is about **NT$13.11 million**.
- **B (grace period and invest):** the loan is still **NT$15 million**, but B now has an investment account.

B's account after five years depends heavily on the return (about NT$29,946 invested each month, roughly NT$1.8 million in total):

| Annual return | Investment account after 5 years | B's net worth vs A |
|---|---|---|
| 7% | about NT$2.14 million | B ahead by about NT$250,000 |
| 4% | about NT$1.99 million | B ahead by about NT$90,000 |
| 0% | about NT$1.80 million | B behind by about NT$100,000 |
| −2% | about NT$1.71 million | B behind by about NT$180,000 |

The table shows:

- **The return must stay above the mortgage rate (2.1% here) for the trade to make sense.** In a flat market you pay extra interest and gain nothing.
- **The upside is smaller than people expect.** Even with five years of 7% returns, B is only about NT$250,000 ahead, while carrying market risk.
- **Timing is the biggest risk.** If markets fall just as the grace period ends, you may have to sell at a loss to cover the suddenly higher payment.

## Why it is poison for many people

All of the above assumes **every dollar saved is actually invested.**

In practice, many people treat the savings as spare money for a car, travel or a nicer renovation. Five years later the payment jumps from NT$26,000 to NT$64,000, with no investments to fall back on. That is the so-called grace period cliff.

The grace period itself is neutral. Discipline decides the outcome.

## When a grace period can make sense

- **You have just received the keys and need to renovate or furnish**, and you have confirmed you can afford the post-grace payment.
- **Your income is expected to rise significantly** (a promotion, or a partner returning to work) and can cover the higher payment.
- **You want to keep an emergency fund** after paying the down payment.
- **You have a clear investment plan and the discipline to follow it**, and can accept the result if markets fall.

## A checklist before applying

1. What share of household income will the post-grace payment take? Be careful above one third.
2. How much would the payment rise if rates went up 1 percentage point?
3. If your investments were down 20% when the grace period ends, could you still make the payment?
4. Is the saved money invested *automatically*, for example by a monthly standing order?

## Conclusion

A grace period loosens cash flow in the early years, but it is not free: you pay more interest and face a higher payment later. Investing the savings can come out slightly ahead when returns stay above the mortgage rate, but you take on market risk to get there.
