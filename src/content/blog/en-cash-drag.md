Many high-earning professionals share the same financial blind spot: ambitious at work, but extremely conservative with their money. Salary lands in the payroll account and stays there; the annual bonus sits in a savings account for years.

This article uses numbers to make one point: **holding too much cash is not "risk-free". It carries a different risk that is easy to miss**, which the finance world calls *cash drag*.

## What is cash drag?

Cash drag happens when a large share of a portfolio sits in cash or low-interest deposits, so that **the overall weighted return is pulled down**, sometimes below inflation.

The calculation is simple. Your overall return is not the return of your best investment. It is the average of every asset, weighted by how much money is in each:

> Weighted return = Σ (amount in each asset × that asset's return) ÷ total assets

Suppose your stock holdings are expected to return 7% a year and your savings account pays 1%. Different cash shares give these results:

| Cash as a share of total assets | Overall weighted return |
|---|---|
| 20% | 5.8% |
| 50% | 4.0% |
| 70% | 2.8% |

Plenty of people feel they invest well because their brokerage account makes 7% a year. But if half their money is in a savings account, their total wealth is only growing at 4%. With long-term inflation around 2% to 2.5%, very little real growth is left.

## Time magnifies the gap

A 2 percentage point difference sounds small, but compounding magnifies it. Take NT$10 million over 20 years:

- At 4% overall: about **NT$21.9 million** after 20 years
- At 6% overall: about **NT$32.1 million** after 20 years

Same starting amount, same 20 years, and a gap of more than NT$10 million from a 2% difference in overall return. That is why cash drag is easy to ignore: your balance never goes down, you simply **earn less**, which is hard to feel.

## Why high earners are especially prone to it

1. **Too busy to deal with money.** Salary arrives automatically and nothing happens next, so cash piles up.
2. **Treating the balance as security.** A growing number feels reassuring, even while purchasing power drains away.
3. **Waiting for a better entry point.** Planning to invest after a dip, then waiting for years.
4. **No defined purpose for each pot of money.** Emergency funds, short-term money and long-term money are mixed together, so everything stays in cash.

## Cash is not the enemy: decide which money *should* be cash

To be clear: **fixing cash drag does not mean investing all your cash.** Some money belongs somewhere safe and instantly accessible:

- **Emergency fund:** usually three to six months of living costs; up to twelve months if your income is irregular or your household has a single earner.
- **Money you will definitely need within three years:** a home down payment, school fees, a wedding or a car. If markets fall, there may be no time to recover.
- **A psychological cushion:** some people need extra cash to sleep well. That is a reasonable need, as long as you know what it costs.

What deserves a second look is **idle cash beyond these needs that has no plan**.

## Check your cash drag in three steps

**Step 1: List every asset.** Stocks, ETFs, funds, time deposits, savings, foreign currency, insurance cash values and property, with current values.

**Step 2: Give each one a reasonable expected return.** Use the actual rate for deposits; for stocks and ETFs use a conservative long-term assumption, not last year's gain.

**Step 3: Look at your overall weighted return and cash share.** Our [portfolio weighted return tool](/portfolio-roi) calculates each asset's share and the weighted return, and flags it when cash is more than 30% of the total. Everything is calculated in your browser and never uploaded.

Then ask yourself three questions:

1. After setting aside the emergency fund and short-term money, how much cash has no clear purpose?
2. Is your overall weighted return above your expected long-term inflation rate? (Our [inflation calculator](/inflation) shows the gap.)
3. If you adjust, how much volatility can you accept?

## Things to watch when you adjust

- **Move gradually.** If you have a lot of idle cash, invest it over several months to reduce the worry of buying at a peak. Try our [DCA calculator](/dca-calculator) to compare approaches.
- **Higher returns usually mean bigger swings.** Raising your overall return also raises the chance of short-term losses, so make sure you can live with them.
- **Diversify rather than concentrate.** The cure for cash drag is not putting everything into one investment, but building an allocation that fits your goals.

At heart, cash drag means your money has not been given a job. Deciding where each pot belongs (emergency, short-term, long-term) is usually the simplest and most effective improvement you can make.
