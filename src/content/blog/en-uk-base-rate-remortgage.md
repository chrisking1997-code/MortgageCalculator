> Written in November 2026. Rates change daily; check current lender offers and the Bank of England's next decision (scheduled for 17 December 2026).

On 5 November 2026 the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1 to raise Bank Rate from 3.75% to **4%**, its first increase since August 2023. The main reason given was inflation, which rose to 3.5% in September, partly driven by higher energy prices.

If you have a mortgage in the UK, how much this affects you depends almost entirely on **what kind of deal you are on**.

## Trackers and variable rates: the change is immediate

Tracker mortgages follow Bank Rate directly, and many standard variable rates (SVRs) move with it. Most lenders passed on the 0.25 percentage point rise, which works out to roughly **£15 a month more per £100,000** of mortgage debt on a typical 25-year repayment loan.

## Fixed deals: nothing changes until the deal ends

If you are partway through a fixed deal, your payment stays the same. But **new fixed rates had already risen** before the announcement, because markets had fully priced in the move. The real impact comes when your fix ends.

## A typical example: a two-year fix coming to an end

Suppose you borrowed **£250,000 over 25 years** on a two-year fix at **4.5%**:

- Monthly payment during the fix: about **£1,390**
- Balance after two years: about **£238,700**

When the fix ends you have three broad outcomes:

| What happens next | New rate (example) | New monthly payment | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| New fix at a competitive rate | 5.5% | about £1,526 | +£136 |
| New fix at a higher rate | 6.0% | about £1,596 | +£207 |
| Do nothing and roll onto SVR | 7.5% | about £1,817 | +£428 |

The rates above are illustrative. The point is the gap: **rolling onto the SVR can cost hundreds of pounds a month more** than arranging a new deal in time.

## A checklist if your deal ends in the next six months

1. **Find your exact end date and any early repayment charges.** Many lenders let you secure a new deal several months before the current one ends.
2. **Compare a product transfer with your current lender and a remortgage elsewhere.** A product transfer is often quicker; a remortgage may be cheaper. Include arrangement fees in the comparison, not just the rate.
3. **Lock in early, keep the option to switch.** Some offers can be replaced if rates fall before completion.
4. **Stress-test your budget.** Check you can afford the payment if rates are 1 percentage point higher when you next refix.
5. **Consider fix length carefully.** Longer fixes give certainty; shorter fixes give flexibility. Neither is automatically better.

## Why Taiwanese readers might care

UK mortgages work very differently from Taiwan's. Most Taiwanese mortgages are floating-rate loans linked to a bank's base rate, so rate changes feed through gradually. In the UK, many borrowers face a **"payment cliff" every two to five years** when a fix ends. We compare the systems in [Taiwan vs US vs UK mortgages](/blog/en-mortgage-systems-compared/).
